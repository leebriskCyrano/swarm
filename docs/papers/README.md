Run `docs/papers/fetch.sh` to download them here (`*.pdf` is git-ignored).

## Wallis & North (1986)

John Joseph Wallis and Douglass C. North, "Measuring the Transaction Sector in
the American Economy, 1870–1970." In Stanley L. Engerman and Robert E. Gallman,
eds., _Long-Term Factors in American Economic Growth_, pp. 95–162. NBER /
University of Chicago Press, 1986.
<https://www.nber.org/chapters/c9679>

The main paper. It builds an operational definition of transaction costs and
sizes the US "transaction sector" from 1870 to 1970.

- **Transaction vs transformation.** Transaction costs are the value of inputs
  (land, labour, capital, entrepreneurship) used to make exchanges.
  Transformation costs are the value of inputs used to turn inputs into
  outputs. Both are ordinary costs that actors trade off at the margin, so
  plain price theory covers them.
- **Buyer and seller sides.** For a buyer, transaction costs are what the buyer
  spends that the seller doesn't receive (search, legal fees, establishing
  credibility). For a seller, they're the costs the seller wouldn't incur if
  selling to themselves (marketing, waiting, title insurance).
- **Timing.** Costs before an exchange are coordinating (information, quality,
  credibility), costs at the exchange are enacting (queues, notaries), and costs
  after it are monitoring (enforcement, inspection, collecting payment).
- **Transaction services vs transaction costs.** Only the part of transaction
  costs bought on the market is observable (lawyers, realtors). Unmarketed
  costs such as search time or waiting can't be seen. The paper measures
  transaction *services*, the way GNP measures market income.
- **Empirical method.**
  1. Transaction industries (intermediaries): count *all* resources used in
     FIRE, wholesale trade and retail trade. Transportation is excluded because
     moving a good transforms it ("couch in the living room").
  2. Transaction occupations in all other industries: count only the *wages* of
     owners and managers, supervisors, foremen and inspectors, clerical workers,
     and purchasing and sales staff. Inside the firm, viewed as a chain of
     contracts from owner to worker, these roles coordinate and monitor.
  3. Government: the same occupational split. Protective services (police,
     guards) are included, though the authors are unsure they belong.
- **Result.** The transaction sector grew from about 25% of GNP in 1870 to
  about 45% in 1970.

## Wallis & North (1988)

John Joseph Wallis and Douglass C. North, "Should Transaction Costs be
Subtracted from Gross National Product?" _The Journal of Economic History_
48(3): 651–654, 1988. <https://www.jstor.org/stable/2121542>

A short follow-up on Kuznets's question of whether transaction services are
intermediate products wrongly counted as final output.

- Table 1 repeats the 1986 estimates by component, as % of GNP, 1870–1970: the
  transaction occupations in non-transaction industries, trade, FIRE, and
  government. In-firm transaction services grew fastest, from about 2% to
  about 10% of GNP.
- Answer: mostly no. In-firm and government transaction services are already
  intermediate inputs, paid as wages. About 40% of trade and FIRE output also
  goes to other industries. Netting out only the final-goods part moves GNP
  growth for 1870–1970 from 3.50% to 3.43% a year, against 3.18% if the whole
  sector were subtracted.
- They read the growth of the sector as evidence for Williamson's and Chandler's
  accounts of the firm.
